\subsection{Baselines in the model}\label{sec:exp-baselines}
Every built-in ran on every suite of its family, on the public scenarios and on a held-out
reference draw (\cref{tab:base-planning,tab:base-control,tab:base-localisation}; \evclass{model},
all runs committed with their hash chains). The tables are generated from the committed runs and
list the suites whose runs are not yet committed. Every built-in planner reaches every goal of
every planning suite, public and held-out: on these maps success does not separate the planners,
and the planning suites rank them by path quality and search effort instead (the leaderboard's
path ratio and expansions). Two further readings recur. Planners that minimise
costmap cost (A*, Dijkstra, D* Lite) return longer paths than breadth-first search, which ignores
cost; path length alone is therefore not a measure of quality here. In control, the Waypoint driver is
the model's own path follower: it drives the global plan without a local costmap or a local
collision check, and it has none of Nav2's failure outcomes, so its success is a reference for the
plan rather than a controller result. DWA, RPP and MPPI each plan in a local window built from the
scan, keep a larger clearance from walls, and can fail as Nav2's controllers do; it is among them
that the suites discriminate. Localisers are separated
by the tier structure: in the localisation runs committed so far, Monte Carlo localisation at the
teaching profile loses its track far more often on hard scenarios than on easy ones.

\textbf{Moving actors.} \texttt{ctrl-dynamic-30} drives each route twice: once with actors that
keep their schedule and once with actors that stop while their next step would walk into the
robot (the scenario rule, not a parameter of the algorithm). The Waypoint driver succeeded on
\DynWaypointWalk{} routes with scheduled actors but \DynWaypointYield{} with yielding ones; all
\DynWaypointYieldTimeouts{} failures were timeouts. With scheduled actors, \DynWaypointWalkContactWins{}
of its successes included contact (up to \DynWaypointWalkMaxContacts{} per drive): blocked by an
actor, it waited until the actor walked on. A yielding actor does not walk on while the robot is
ahead of it, and a driver without a local planner cannot go round, so both wait; this reading
follows from the model's rules and the recorded contacts, but was not traced tick by tick
(\evclass{inference}). The local controllers were nearly insensitive to the rule (DWA
\DynDwaWalk{} vs \DynDwaYield{}, RPP \DynRppWalk{} vs \DynRppYield{}, MPPI \DynMppiWalk{} vs
\DynMppiYield{}). Success alone would rank the contact-making driver first; the leaderboard
therefore reports contacts beside success.

\subsection{The same planner in the model and in ROS~2}\label{sec:exp-r27}
We exported the A* starter file to a ROS~2 node and asked it and Nav2's stock planner (Smac 2D)
for paths on the same requests from the live global costmap in Gazebo: \StackPlanRequests{}
requests (\StackPlanScenarios{} Depot scenarios, \StackPlanSessions{} fresh sessions). The exported file found
\StackAstarPlans{}, stock Nav2 \StackStockPlans{}. This is a convention difference, not a better
planner: Nav2's planner server refuses a start or goal inside inflated cost, whereas \cocolab{}
(and therefore the exported node) first moves them to the nearest free cell. We therefore also
report Nav2 with the same snapping applied to its requests (\cref{sec:exp-gap}).

\subsection{Case study: an any-angle planner that wins in the model and loses in ROS~2}
A community submission, Lazy Theta* \cite{nash2010lazytheta}, was verified on the leaderboard
in the sandbox. As a ROS~2 node it planned \StackThetaPlans{} requests in Gazebo, but with
Nav2's Regulated Pure Pursuit controller \cite{macenski2023rpp} it reached \StackThetaDrives{}
goals, against \StackStockDrives{} for stock Nav2 and \StackAstarDrives{} for the exported A*
starter on the same \StackDriveScenarios{} drives. When first observed, this was attributed, as an
inference, to the any-angle paths passing close to obstacles, where the controller's collision
check stops the robot. We report the negative result rather than remove it, and test the
inference by recording the costmap cost under every path the controller received
(\cref{sec:exp-gap}).

\subsection{A user controller through the real stack}
The exported pure-pursuit starter replaced Nav2's controller behind COCO's command arbiter on
the \StackPPRounds{} fetch rounds of the colour Depot, \StackPPRunsPerRound{} runs each: \StackPPLegs{} legs reached. Paired
with the model on the same legs, the median difference in time per leg was \StackPPGapS{}~s
(\StackPPGapN{} legs).

\textbf{The snapping convention.} Over \SnapSessions{} further sessions on the same requests,
Nav2 as given found \SnapStockFound{} paths; with start and goal snapped to the nearest free cell
of its live costmap (the rule the model uses) it found \SnapSnappedFound{}, as did the exported A*
starter (\SnapAstarFound{}). Snapping moved a start or goal by more than one cell only on the
\SnapMovedScenarios{} scenarios stock Nav2 refused; on one of them the goal lies
\SnapMaxGoalM{}~m from free space in Gazebo's costmap although the model drew it with clearance,
a discrepancy we report as unresolved. Snapped, Nav2's paths were slightly shorter than the
starter's (paired median \SnapPairedGapM{}~m).

\textbf{Why the any-angle planner fails under Nav2} (\cref{fig:corner}). Re-running the \StackDriveScenarios{} drives while recording,
for every plan the controller received, the raw cost under each pose in the live local costmap:
with Lazy Theta* (\CostThetaReached{} reached), \CostThetaMinPct{}--\CostThetaMaxPct{}\,\% of
the received plans put at least one pose in inscribed or lethal cost on each drivable scenario,
against at most \CostControlMaxPct{}\,\% for stock Nav2 (\CostStockReached{}) and the A* starter
(\CostAstarReached{}) on the same drives. This supports the corner-cutting explanation; that the
controller's collision check is what stops each drive remains an inference, as its decisions were
not logged.
